Choosing Grace

October 2026 6 min read

Let’s talk about grace.

Some days I feel like I’m living in a perpetual state of failure. Socrates said that the unexamined life is not worth living. It’s one of my favorite quotes, and I try to apply it to my life in a tangible way. I reflect. I journal, not as often as I should, but regularly enough. Regularly enough to know that I often feel like a failure.

That might be too harsh, so let me reframe it: I routinely miss the mark.

That reframe matters more than it seems. Failure sounds final, like a verdict on who you are. Missing the mark is something that happens to someone who is still aiming. You can’t miss a target you were never reaching for. Examination cuts both ways, though. Look closely enough at your life and you’ll always find something worth working on. The trouble starts when the looking turns into a trial where you’re always the defendant and the verdict is always guilty.

These are the questions I ask myself at the end of the day. Was I kind today? Did I forgive someone who needed it, whether or not I felt they deserved it? Did I show grace when a situation called for it? When someone had earned the full weight of my anger, did I choose meekness instead? Did I eat well? Was I productive? The list goes on and on. No matter what I do, it seems I’m not measuring up to the person I so badly want to be.

Those are good questions, and I’m not going to stop asking them. But I’ve noticed that I rarely ask the other ones. What did I get right today? Where did I show up even though I didn’t feel like it? What did I handle better than I would have a year ago? When the audit only looks for deficits, deficits are all it will ever find.

What makes it worse is the comparison game. I know people who are far busier than I am, and somehow they still work out, read regularly, and keep learning and growing. They show up when they’re needed. They find time to volunteer. Why can’t I be like that? Why does my best never feel like enough? And where are they finding the time?

Here’s what I have to keep reminding myself: I’m comparing my inside to their outside. I know every one of my shortcuts, every skipped workout, every night I chose the couch. I see none of theirs. I don’t see what they’ve given up to make room for everything they do, what support they have that I don’t, or what they’re quietly struggling with that never comes up in conversation. Comparison measures your whole story against a few highlights of someone else’s, and the scale is rigged before you ever step on it.

Even when the comparison is accurate, it’s rarely useful. Someone else’s capacity was built in a different season, with different resources, carrying different weight. Their pace isn’t your assignment. If watching someone inspires you to take one concrete step, take it. If it only leaves you feeling small, that’s your cue to look away and come back to your own lane.

Some days I get off work after ten or twelve hours and I’m so exhausted that all I want is to go home and rest. But if I rest, the guilt follows me all the way to bed and is waiting for me when I wake up. I have bills. I should be out doing gig work or hustling in some fashion, turning every waking hour into money instead of sitting still.

Maybe you know that feeling. It’s the voice that says rest is something you have to earn, and you haven’t earned it yet. The problem is that the bar for earning it keeps moving. There’s always one more task, one more dollar, one more thing someone more disciplined would have done. So you either push through on empty, or you rest without actually resting: your body on the couch while your mind sits in the courtroom.

Neither one works. Running on empty doesn’t make you more productive. It makes you sloppier, shorter with the people you love, and closer to a breakdown you can’t schedule around. And guilty rest doesn’t restore anything. If you’re going to stop, stop fully. Rest isn’t the opposite of progress. It’s part of what makes progress possible.

This cycle won’t end until we decide we’re not going to stay stuck in it. That takes a paradigm shift, and it starts with grace. Not only the grace we extend to other people, but the grace we extend to ourselves.

I believe most people are doing the best they can, handling their lives the way they’re best equipped to. I say that easily about others. When a friend is overwhelmed, I don’t call him lazy. When a coworker drops the ball after a brutal week, I don’t assume it reveals something about his character. I give the benefit of the doubt without thinking twice. Yet when I’m the one who comes up short, I hold myself to a standard I would never put on anyone else. If most people are doing the best they can with what they have, that includes me. And it includes you.

The next part of the shift is awareness: understanding what’s actually happening in your life and being honest about it. If something isn’t working for you, whether that’s a job, a relationship, or a routine, you need to name it and start working to change it. I know, easier said than done. But if you want to move to another level, this step is essential. You can’t conquer what you don’t confront, and you can’t confront what you don’t identify.

This is where the journaling and self-examination earn their keep. Their purpose was never to build a case against yourself. It’s to gather information. Maybe the reason you can’t stay consistent at the gym isn’t a lack of discipline. Maybe your schedule genuinely leaves no room, and the fix is a twenty-minute workout at home, not more willpower. Maybe the reason you keep snapping at people isn’t that you’re a bad person. Maybe you’re running on five hours of sleep and haven’t eaten since noon. When you look at your life like an investigator instead of a prosecutor, the question changes from “What’s wrong with me?” to “What’s actually going on here, and what can I change?” The first question leads to shame. The second one leads somewhere.

Let’s circle back to the grace we owe ourselves, because it’s easy to misunderstand. Showing yourself grace doesn’t mean ignoring the problems in your life or avoiding the serious internal conversations about changes you know you need to make. Grace isn’t an excuse, and it isn’t lowering the bar. It means choosing to forgive yourself instead of beating yourself up or dwelling on the miss. It also means taking the necessary steps toward growth and actively pursuing the person you are becoming.

In fact, grace is what makes real change sustainable. Shame might get you moving for a day or two, but it’s a terrible long-term fuel. When every slip turns into a referendum on your worth, you eventually stop looking altogether. You stop journaling, stop reflecting, stop asking the hard questions, because the answers hurt too much. Grace keeps that door open. It lets you look honestly at where you fell short, because you know you won’t be crushed by what you find.

So what does that look like on an ordinary day? It might be closing out the evening by naming one thing you did well before you name anything you’d change. It might be catching the harsh voice in your head and asking whether you’d say the same thing to a friend in your position. It might be trading “I should have” for “next time I will.” And it might be giving yourself permission to rest on the nights you genuinely have nothing left, without putting yourself on trial afterward.

Extend that grace to yourself every day, because as I said at the start, you will regularly feel like you’re falling short. There will be days when you don’t have the bandwidth to do one more thing. That happens to everyone from time to time, so don’t fixate on the miss. Focus on what you did accomplish. Identify what, if anything, can be improved. Put systems in place so it’s easier next time. Then move on.

Missing the mark doesn’t disqualify you. It just means you’re still aiming. Grace gives you the breathing room to try again tomorrow.
