Fuel

What You Eat Is Affecting How You Think

September 2026 7 min read

I want to be upfront about something before we get into this one: I'm not a nutritionist, and this isn't a pillar I've mastered. If anything, it's the one I'm furthest behind on. But that's actually the reason I'm writing it. Aeilus was never supposed to be five areas where I already have it figured out, handing down advice from the finish line. It's five areas I'm actually working on — and this is one where I'm still very much in the learning phase.

So consider this me thinking out loud, with the research in front of me, instead of pretending I got here some other way.

The Brain-Food Connection, Plainly

Your brain doesn't get a day off. It's running your thoughts, your mood, your motor function, your senses — constantly, even while you sleep — and all of that requires fuel. That fuel comes from what you eat. Harvard Health puts it simply: what you eat directly affects the structure and function of your brain, and ultimately your mood.

Part of why is the gut. Roughly 95% of your body's serotonin — the neurotransmitter tied to mood, sleep, and appetite — is produced in your gastrointestinal tract, not your brain. Your gut is lined with hundreds of millions of neurons, and the bacteria living there directly influence how those neurons function. Diets high in refined sugar and processed food have been linked, in multiple studies, to increased inflammation and worse outcomes for mood disorders like depression. Diets closer to Mediterranean or traditional patterns — more vegetables, fruit, unprocessed grains, fish — are associated with a 25 to 35% lower risk of depression compared to a typical Western diet.

None of that means food is the whole story. But it's a bigger piece of the story than most of us were ever taught.

Signs You Might Be Under-Fueling Your Brain, Not Just Your Body

A few signs this pillar might need attention:

  • Afternoon energy crashes that hit hard, especially after eating
  • Trouble concentrating or "brain fog" that comes and goes with no clear cause
  • Mood swings that seem to track with when and what you last ate
  • Feeling foggy or irritable and reaching for caffeine or sugar to push through it
  • Headaches or fatigue that improve noticeably after drinking water

I've had all five of these at different points, usually without connecting them back to what I'd eaten that day.

What the Research Actually Points To

This isn't a diet plan — I'm not qualified to hand you one, and honestly, that's not the point of Aeilus anyway. But a few things are consistently backed by research.

Blood sugar stability matters more than any single food. Sharp glucose spikes — usually from refined carbs and sugar — followed by crashes are linked to fatigue, poor concentration, and irritability. Eating more consistently, with protein and fiber alongside carbs, helps smooth that curve out.

Hydration affects your brain faster than you'd expect. A controlled study published in the British Journal of Nutrition found that mild dehydration — as little as 1.5% of body weight in fluid loss — measurably slowed working memory and increased fatigue and anxiety in healthy young men. You don't have to be seriously dehydrated to feel the effects.

Omega-3s show up again and again in brain health research. A 2022 systematic review of clinical trials found consistent links between omega-3 intake (from fish, walnuts, flaxseed) and measurable effects on brain function, though researchers note the research is still developing and not every study agrees on the size of the effect.

None of this requires an overhaul. It just requires attention — noticing how you feel after you eat, the way you'd notice how you feel after a workout or a bad night's sleep.

Go Deeper

I'm not the authority here, so don't take my word as the last word. A few real sources if you want to read further:

  • Harvard Health — Nutritional Psychiatry: Your Brain on Food
  • Mild dehydration impairs cognitive performance and mood of men — British Journal of Nutrition
  • Effects of Omega-3 Polyunsaturated Fatty Acids on Brain Functions — systematic review, Cureus

Where I'm At

I'm not going to pretend I've fixed this pillar by writing an article about it. I still eat on the run more than I should. I still forget to drink water until my head starts hurting. But I'm paying attention to it now in a way I wasn't a year ago, and that's the next step — not the whole staircase.

If this is a weak spot for you too, you're not behind. You're just starting where I am.
