Connection

Loneliness Doesn't Always Look Like Being Alone

July 2026 5 min read

Let's talk about loneliness.

We're told that in an age of social media and constant connection we've somehow become more disconnected than ever. At this point, that's almost a cliché. We all know it. We all repeat it. We all feel it. Yet very little changes.

It's easy to blame our phones, but I don't think they're the real problem. They're just the symptom.

We refuse to put them down long enough to notice what's happening around us. To notice the people sitting beside us. To notice ourselves.

Every day we find ourselves standing in lines, sitting in waiting rooms, riding elevators, or walking through stores. Moments that could invite conversation, reflection, or even a simple smile have become opportunities to disappear behind a screen.

I'm guilty of it too.

I'll look around for a moment and notice that almost everyone else is doing the same thing. Heads down. Eyes fixed on glowing rectangles. Nobody makes eye contact. Nobody says hello. It's as if we've all silently agreed to pretend the people around us don't exist.

But I don't think it's because we don't want connection.

I think it's because connection requires vulnerability.

Putting your phone away means allowing yourself to be seen. It means risking awkwardness. It means admitting that maybe your life isn't as exciting as you want everyone else to believe. It means sitting alone with your thoughts instead of drowning them in endless scrolling.

If I'm honest, there are times when I keep scrolling even when there's nothing worth looking at.

Not because I'm interested in what's on my screen, but because I'm afraid of what it says if I'm not.

Somewhere deep inside, I still wrestle with the feeling that I've carried for most of my life: that I'm not enough. That I'm the outsider. The loser. The one nobody would choose to talk to anyway.

So I scroll.

I project the image that I'm busy. That I'm important. That I have somewhere else I'd rather be and something more interesting demanding my attention.

It's a performance.

And I have a feeling I'm not the only one acting.

Every once in a while, I decide I won't play along. I put the phone away and tell myself I'm going to be present. I look around the room, hoping to catch someone's eye.

Almost no one looks up.

For a moment, the loneliness becomes louder than it was before. The silence feels heavier. Eventually, I reach for my phone again, not because I want it, but because it hides the emptiness.

That's the strange thing about loneliness.

It convinces us to build the very walls we desperately wish someone would climb.

Maybe the answer isn't deleting every social media app or throwing our phones into the ocean.

Maybe it starts with one small act of courage.

Looking up.

Smiling first.

Saying hello.

Asking someone how they're doing and actually waiting for the answer.

Someone has to be the first person willing to risk a little awkwardness so someone else can experience a little less loneliness.

Perhaps the person standing next to you isn't ignoring you because they don't care.

Perhaps they're hoping you'll be brave enough to make the first move.

Loneliness doesn't disappear because we're surrounded by people. It disappears when someone reminds us that we're no longer invisible.
