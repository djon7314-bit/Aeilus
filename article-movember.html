Body

One Small Decision

November 2026 6 min read

Nicole “Nikki.” She had the best laugh.

My cousin Nicole was thirty when she died. Everyone called her Nikki.

She had found a lump in her breast. She knew it was there. And for reasons I’ll never fully understand (fear, busyness, maybe the quiet hope that if you don’t look at something it isn’t real), she didn’t get it checked. By the time she did, the decision that could have changed everything had already been made. She just didn’t know she was making it.

Nikki had the best laugh. It was one of those infectious laughs that pulled everyone in. You’d find yourself laughing right along with her whether or not you knew what was funny.

And there was the thing she did her whole life. Lots of kids suck their thumb when they’re little. Nikki sucked her index and middle fingers, and she never stopped, while her other hand twirled her hair. Whenever she was relaxing or watching TV, that’s how you’d find her. I’ll always remember that.

I have thirteen cousins on my mom’s side. Of all of them, Nikki and her sister Monique, who we called Momo, were the two my older brother and I were closest to. We were all close in age, and our moms lived within blocks of each other for most of our lives. I loved every minute I got to spend with the three of them.

Now all three are gone: my brother, Momo, and Nikki. Each of them left too young.

With every family member I lose, I stop and ask the same question: what could we have done, and what could they have done, to still be here? In Nikki’s case, the answer is painfully simple. The warning was right there. She needed to acknowledge it and act on it.

Cancer runs through my family. I’ve lost more people to it than I like to count. And if all those losses have taught me one thing, it’s this: most of the time, you don’t get to decide whether it shows up. But you almost always get a say in when you find out.

That’s what this month is really about.

The number

Every November, men grow moustaches for Movember. The moustache was never the point. It’s the excuse. It’s there so somebody asks about it, and a conversation starts that men usually avoid.

So here’s a number to start one: 33.

That’s the average age a man is diagnosed with testicular cancer. Not 63. Thirty-three. It’s a young man’s cancer, hitting at the age when most of us are building careers, starting families, and still quietly convinced we’re untouchable.

And it’s one of the most treatable cancers there is. Caught while it’s still contained, the five-year survival rate is about 99 percent. Still, the American Cancer Society expects around 630 men in the U.S. to die from it this year.

The space between a disease that’s almost always beatable and the men who still die from it is where the small decision lives.

The first sign is often a lump or swelling. Usually a painless one. Which is exactly the kind of thing a man will tell himself to ignore.

Why we wait

It isn’t the specific cancer that stays with me. It’s the outcome, and how much of it hangs on one choice made early or made late.

Men are good at waiting. We were raised to push through, to not make a fuss, to treat the doctor like a mechanic you only call when something’s already broken. We’ll check the oil in the truck before we check ourselves. Somewhere along the way, “I’m fine” became a personality.

I know that pattern because I lived it. For years I was the protector, the one making sure everyone else was okay. The thing nobody tells you about being the protector is that you’re usually the last person you protect.

Waiting feels like strength. It isn’t. Usually it’s just fear wearing a calm face.

So I’ll be honest: I’m writing this to myself as much as to you. I put off my own checkup too. Like a lot of men, I let my work schedule dictate everything else in my life, and my health kept landing at the bottom of the list. Prioritizing work over everything has cost me, and I can see that now. My appointment is finally locked in. I’m changing slowly, but I’m changing, because nobody else is going to make that change for me. It’s on me.

I know I’m not alone in that. For a lot of men, the work always wins. The shift, the deadline, the business that needs one more hour. We tell ourselves we’ll go when things slow down, and things never slow down. Our health quietly slides to the bottom of the list, below everything that pays.

And sometimes it isn’t about time at all. It’s about money. If you can’t afford to keep health insurance, you probably can’t afford to pay out of pocket for a checkup either. So you skip it. That isn’t a character flaw. It’s math, and a lot of men are doing it. In 2024, nearly four in ten uninsured adults said they delayed or skipped care they needed because of cost, according to KFF. That’s more than twice the rate of people with private coverage.

But skipping the checkup doesn’t make the cost go away. It just moves it down the road, where it’s usually bigger, and sometimes it’s paid in something money can’t replace.

The one small decision

This isn’t a seven-step plan. It’s one decision, made this month.

  • Know what’s normal for your body. If something changes, like a lump, swelling, a heaviness, or an ache that won’t leave, don’t wait it out. Get it looked at.
  • Book the appointment you’ve been putting off. Ask your doctor which checks are right for your age and your family history. If cancer runs in your family the way it runs in mine, say so out loud.
  • Tell one man. Movember’s ask this year is to do it together. Text a brother, a cousin, a friend. Not “you good?” Something real: When’s the last time you actually got checked?

If cost is what’s been stopping you, you have more options than you might think. Community health centers charge on a sliding scale based on what you earn, whether or not you have insurance. You can find one near you at findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov. And if you do have insurance, check your plan, because many cover a yearly preventive visit at no cost to you. Don’t let the price of a checkup decide for you before you’ve even looked.

The appointment takes an hour. Not knowing can take everything.

The decision is still yours

My cousin doesn’t get to make that decision again.

You do.

Make it this month.
